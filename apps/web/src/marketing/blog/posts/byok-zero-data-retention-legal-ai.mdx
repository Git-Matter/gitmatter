Every legal AI feature, whether review, redlining, extraction, or drafting, works the
same way underneath: client documents are sent to an AI model, and text comes back. The
legal questions that matter all live in that one sentence. Whose model? Under what
terms? And what happens to the document after the answer comes back?

Two phrases keep coming up when firms try to answer those questions: "bring your own
key" and "zero data retention." They describe different controls, with different
limits. This guide explains them in plain English and gives you the questions to ask
any legal AI vendor.

## The usual arrangement, and why it worries firms

Most AI tools, from consumer chat apps to many legal platforms, run on the vendor's own
account with the AI provider. Your documents go to the vendor, the vendor sends them on
to the AI company under the vendor's agreement, and results come back. Three concerns
follow.

**Storage terms you never negotiated.** Consumer AI subscriptions typically keep chat
history and may use content to improve their services. When a lawyer pastes an
agreement into a personal chat account, the firm has effectively handed client material
to a third party under terms nobody at the firm has read.

**An extra party in the chain.** A confidentiality analysis has to cover every hand a
document passes through. When the vendor sits between you and the AI company, that is
one more party holding client material.

**The training question.** Services differ in whether they use submitted content to
improve models. A no-training policy also does not mean that requests are never stored.
Check training, storage, safety logging, and deletion separately.

Assess those terms against your firm's client instructions and confidentiality
requirements before submitting material.

## What "bring your own key" means

An API key authorizes requests under an account with a provider such as Anthropic
(Claude), Google (Gemini), or OpenAI. Supplying your firm's key lets a legal platform
send requests under that account.

With bring your own key (BYOK), requests using your saved key run under your firm's
provider account. Check whether the platform can also use a server key or another
provider when that key is removed. Three controls deserve attention:

1. **Your provider terms.** Review the agreement and settings for the service, model,
   and account used. A consumer assistant subscription and a provider API account can
   have different data policies.
2. **Your choice of provider.** Firms have preferences, and sometimes client
   instructions, about where data may go. With your own key, the firm picks the
   provider and can switch later.
3. **Your revocation control.** Revoking a key at the provider blocks future requests
   authorized by that key. It does not stop other providers, connected assistants, or
   requests already in progress. Removing a saved key from gitmatter can make a
   configured server key active.

One practical note: a platform that holds your key must store it carefully. The key
should be stored encrypted, never written into logs, and never visible in the browser.
Ask about all three.

## What "zero data retention" means

Zero data retention (ZDR) is a provider arrangement that limits routine retention of
covered request content. Its scope depends on the account, model, features, and terms.
Provider policies can include metadata, caching, safety, or legal exceptions.

For example, OpenAI's `store: false` request setting does not establish account-level
ZDR. OpenRouter's data-collection filter is separate from its ZDR routing controls and
Safety Retention settings. Review [gitmatter's AI data-handling guide](/docs/admin/ai-data-handling)
for dated provider sources and the controls the application sends. A ZDR label does
not establish a legal outcome or guarantee that no copy exists anywhere.

### What zero data retention does not do

Honest limits, because vendors often blur them:

- A provider agreement covers only the services and data within its scope. gitmatter
  still stores your documents and work history. Connected assistants and external tools
  have separate policies.
- It does not make the AI's output correct. It answers where data goes, not whether
  the work is good. The duty of competence is untouched.
- A key does not verify an account's retention arrangement. Confirm coverage with the
  provider, including model and feature restrictions.

## The full picture: your key, provider terms, and a record

Confidentiality is about where data goes. Accountability is about what was done with
it. A defensible AI setup needs both:

- **Your own key** identifies the provider account authorizing the request.
- **Verified provider controls** establish which training and retention terms apply.
- **An audit trail** makes changes attributable and reviewable within the matter.

Keep those records together: the service and account used, the applicable data policy,
and the changes made. BYOK and an audit history support that assessment; neither
establishes zero retention or legal compliance on its own.

## Eight questions to ask any legal AI vendor

1. Whose account with the AI provider processes our documents, ours or yours?
2. If yours: under what storage and training terms, and can we read them?
3. If ours: how is our key stored? Encrypted? Ever written to logs?
4. Can we choose the AI provider, and switch later?
5. Is zero data retention set up, and where is that documented?
6. Where do you store our documents, and how are they encrypted?
7. Is every AI action on a matter recorded with a name, the exact change, and the
   reason?
8. Can we run the software on our own servers if a client requires it?

A vendor comfortable with legal customers will have crisp answers to all eight.
